\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Stacking is a fundamental problem with applications such as bin packing, industrial warehouse storage, and stone-wall construction.
In robotic bin packing, container walls can provide lateral support~\cite{wang2022binpacking}.
Without such support, a freestanding structure of $n$ irregular objects must approximate a prescribed target shape $\target\subset\real^3$ while maintaining force and torque equilibrium under gravity through inter-object and ground contacts.
Satisfying these requirements is challenging because pose changes of irregular objects alter both occupied space and contact locations and normals, coupling geometric fit with force and torque equilibrium across the entire structure.
We minimize unfilled target volume subject to physical feasibility:
\begin{gather}
\min_{q_1,\cdots,q_n\in\SE(3)} \vol\left(\target\backslash\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\object_i(q_i)\right)\quad\text{s.t.} \label{eq:problem_definition}\\
c_\text{phys}(q_1,\cdots,q_n)=0. \nonumber
\end{gather}
where $\object_i(q_i)\subset\real^3$ is the region occupied by object $i$ at pose $q_i\in\SE(3)$, $\vol(\cdot)$ denotes volume, and $c_\text{phys}=0$ represents stability and non-interpenetration constraints.
Constructing such a structure also requires selecting objects, determining which objects support one another, and deciding in what order to place them.
Each placement changes the remaining inventory, available space, and support for subsequent objects, so a currently stable placement may limit the final target volume filling.
These interdependent discrete choices make sequence planning combinatorial, and the iterative contact solves required for each candidate make the search computationally expensive~\cite{furrer2017stacking}.

For freestanding stone walls, Johns et al.~\cite{johns2020autonomous,johns2023framework} combine masonry heuristics, contact force optimization, geometric alignment, and simulation; Liu and Napp~\cite{liu2023stability} use heuristic filtering and simulation-based structural strength assessments.
These methods assess stability but do not use equilibrium across the entire structure to guide continuous pose refinement or explicitly compare alternative placement sequences.
Menezes et al.~\cite{menezes2021rocks} learn placement values reflecting future rewards, with fixed object sequences and orientations.

We propose a GPU-accelerated framework that plans placement sequences for irregular stones using Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).
Within each expansion, bilevel pose optimization refines the candidate placement and GPU-accelerated subADMM simulation~\cite{ji2025gpu} evaluates the stability of the resulting stack.
Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary_result} shows preliminary results for several target shapes in simulation and a stone stack built outdoors. For the outdoor experiment, we ran the planner on the CPU because CUDA was unavailable on the platform.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} illustrates how MCTS uses the simulated configurations to compare subsequent placements.

In our bilevel pose optimization, the lower level solves for shared contact forces across the stack. At the upper level, differentiable support functions (DSFs)~\cite{lee2023uncertain,an2024riemannianmtd} provide the contact geometry derivatives needed to refine the candidate pose for geometric fit and structure-wide equilibrium.
This formulation builds on contact-force-based manipulation and grasp optimization~\cite{lee2025contactfactorgraph,zurbrugg2025graspqp,chen2025bodex}, extending the optimization to the coupled equilibrium of the growing stack.
In particular, BODex~\cite{chen2025bodex} uses finite differences for coarse contact derivatives and fixed link-frame contact coordinates during fine-stage differentiation while we differentiate DSF contact geometry for pose updates.

Liu and Napp~\cite{liu2023stability} use simulated shake tests to select the candidate pose for which the partially built wall withstands shaking the longest.
In our framework, MCTS simulates settling for each refined candidate that passes the equilibrium check and explores subsequent placements from the resulting configuration.
The choice of the current placement thus accounts for both the response of the stack to disturbances and how much of the target later placements can fill.

Exploring these continuations requires repeated contact solves across branches. PMBS~\cite{huang2022pmbs} batches simulations within MCTS for clutter retrieval. In stacking, each branch requires contact force optimization to refine the candidate pose, followed by simulation that resolves frictional contact at every time step as the stack settles.
Within each stack, bodywise ADMM updates local contact force copies in parallel and enforces agreement at shared contacts.
We therefore batch both bilevel pose optimization and simulation across candidate stacks on the GPU.
Our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}]
\item A novel differentiable bilevel pose optimization formulation that incorporates both geometric fit and force and torque equilibrium across the entire structure into the candidate pose gradient, a coupling absent from the prior stone-stacking methods discussed above.
\item To our knowledge, the first MCTS planner for freestanding stacks of irregular stones, selecting each placement by comparing feasible continuations from the stack configurations predicted by settling simulations.
\item GPU-accelerated MCTS evaluation through bodywise ADMM for parallel contact force optimization within each stack and batched bilevel pose optimization and simulation across candidates.
\end{itemize}
